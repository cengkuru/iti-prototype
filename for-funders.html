For funders and investors

The evidence behind the score, and its limits.

The ITI supports fiduciary-risk conversations and reform decisions. This page sets out the methodology, the quality assurance, the cost, and what the ITI does and does not claim, so a funder can judge its rigour directly.

The assurance case

What stands behind a number.

Six things a funder should be able to check before relying on an assessment.

Methodology

A fixed, published framework

Every score is built from indicators that roll up into sub-variables, variables, four dimensions, and one national score. The framework is the same in every country, so results are comparable rather than bespoke.

Data source

Primary, country-level evidence

Assessments draw on a country's own laws, institutional records, disclosure portals, and published procurement and project data, reviewed against the framework rather than self-reported by the entity being assessed.

Quality assurance

Reviewed, not asserted

Scoring is documented at each level, so any number can be traced back to its evidence and re-checked. An assurance step verifies sources and scoring before a result is published.

Use cases

Risk, reform, and benchmarking

Funders use the ITI to inform fiduciary-risk conversations, target reform support at the weakest dimension, and benchmark transparency across a portfolio or region on one scale.

Limits

What it does not claim

The ITI is not a credit rating, a corruption measure, or a verdict on a government. It measures transparency systems, not project outcomes or value-for-money directly. It informs due diligence; it does not replace it.

Cost and value

Low marginal cost to scale

A country assessment is modest relative to the infrastructure spending it scrutinises, and the marginal cost falls as the method and tooling are reused. Specific figures are in the evidence ledger below, pending verification.

Where it fits

The ITI complements the tools funders already use.

It does not duplicate the IMF PIMA, the World Bank InfraGov / IGA, or OECD instruments. It measures a different thing: whether transparency works in practice, at the project level.

Apply the ITI

What applying the ITI in a country involves.

A clear pathway: who does what, roughly how long it takes, and the support available at each step.

Scoping and agreement

Lead: Government and CoSTIndicative: 4 to 8 weeks

Confirm the assessment scope, data access, and the local team. CoST and the ITI-Core team provide the framework and onboarding.

Data collection

Lead: Local assessment teamIndicative: 8 to 12 weeks

Gather the laws, institutional records, disclosure data, and project information the framework requires, with quality checks against the source.

Scoring and assurance

Lead: Assessors and ITI-CoreIndicative: 4 to 6 weeks

Score against the framework, document the evidence at each level, and run the assurance step before any number is finalised.

Publication and use

Lead: CoST and partnersIndicative: ongoing

Publish the result, brief stakeholders, and use the dimension breakdown to target reform. Re-assessment shows whether the score moved.

Support model

The ITI-Core team maintains the framework, trains assessors, and provides the assurance backbone, so a country is not building the method from scratch. This is what keeps the marginal cost of each new assessment low as the ITI scales.
